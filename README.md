# Customer Behavior Intelligence Engine

A development-ready data science project analyzing customer transaction
behavior: RFM+ feature engineering, K-Means customer segmentation, and a
leakage-safe purchase-propensity model (Logistic Regression).

---

## Project Overview

This project builds an end-to-end customer analytics pipeline: synthetic
data generation → cleaning → RFM+ feature engineering → EDA → K-Means
segmentation → a temporally leakage-safe purchase-propensity model →
statistical interpretation → browser-based dashboard.

## Business Problem

- Who are our customers, and how do their spending patterns differ?
- Can we group customers into a small number of interpretable, actionable
  segments?
- Which customers are likely to make another purchase in the next 30 days,
  and which behavioral signals predict that?

## Why This Problem Matters

Acquiring a new customer typically costs more than retaining an existing
one. Segmenting customers by value/engagement and identifying who is likely
to buy again (or at risk of churning) lets a business target retention
spend, personalize outreach, and prioritize high-value relationships.

## Dataset

**Synthetic**, generated by `src/generate_data.py` (~54,000 transactions,
~5,100 customers with at least one transaction, fixed random seed). Each
customer is assigned a hidden "purchase archetype" (one-time, occasional,
regular, frequent, VIP) driving realistic heterogeneity in frequency and
spend. Includes realistic **missing values**, **duplicate rows**, and
**quantity anomalies** (implausible bulk-order-style spikes).

Columns: `transaction_id, customer_id, product_id, category,
transaction_date, quantity, unit_price, discount, payment_method, region,
revenue`

> Raw/processed CSVs are **not** committed (see `.gitignore`) — regenerate
> locally with the commands below.

## Data Preprocessing

Documented in `src/data_cleaning.py`: duplicates dropped; negative prices
(entry errors) corrected via absolute value; rows still missing price
dropped; missing discount treated as 0; missing `payment_method`/`region`
labeled `"Unknown"` (kept, not dropped, since they're contextual categorical
fields); extreme quantity anomalies winsorized at the 99.9th percentile.

## EDA

`src/eda.py` produces 7 charts: monetary value distribution, frequency
distribution, recency distribution, AOV vs. frequency, a customer-revenue
concentration (Pareto) curve, preferred-category counts, and regional
revenue.

## Customer Feature Engineering (RFM+)

`src/customer_features.py` builds, per customer: `recency`, `frequency`,
`monetary_value`, `average_order_value`, `total_quantity`,
`category_diversity`, `average_discount`, `purchase_frequency`
(transactions per active month), `active_months`, `tenure_days`,
`preferred_category`, `preferred_payment`.

## Customer Segmentation

`src/segmentation.py` uses **K-Means**: log-transform skewed monetary
features → standardize → evaluate **K = 2 to 8** by silhouette score (not
an arbitrary choice) → profile the chosen cluster set using the *original*
(unscaled) feature values, and derive human-readable segment names directly
from those cluster statistics (see `name_segments()` — never invented).

The silhouette score in this dataset is maximized at a small K, reflecting
a population that splits primarily into an engaged/high-value group and a
larger disengaged group. The notebook documents this explicitly and notes
that a business team wanting more granular targeting could reasonably
choose a higher K (e.g. 4) at a modest cost in cluster separation — a
real trade-off between statistical clarity and business actionability.

## Purchase Propensity

**Target:** did the customer purchase again within the next 30 days after a
fixed cutoff date (`FEATURE_CUTOFF_DATE` in `src/utils.py`)?

**Leakage prevention (important):** features are computed using ONLY
transactions on/before the cutoff date; the label uses ONLY transactions
strictly after the cutoff, within the following 30 days. These two windows
never overlap — see `src/propensity_model.py` for the exact implementation.
This is a temporal methodology, not a random split, because in a real
deployment you would only ever have past data to predict a future outcome.

## Machine Learning

`src/modeling.py` trains a **Logistic Regression** (scikit-learn) on the
leakage-safe propensity dataset (recency, frequency, monetary_value, AOV,
category diversity, average discount, purchase_frequency, active_months,
tenure_days), using a stratified train/test split (rows are independent
customer snapshots at one cutoff, so a random split is appropriate at the
row level even though the *label itself* is temporally defined).

Evaluated with **confusion matrix, precision, recall, F1, and ROC-AUC** —
never accuracy alone. See the notebook's Section 11 for the discussion of
false-positive vs. false-negative costs and why a business might prefer a
different decision threshold than the default 0.5.

## Statistical Analysis

`src/statistical_analysis.py` fits a `statsmodels` **Logit** model on
standardized features, reporting coefficients, standard errors, p-values,
confidence intervals, and odds ratios. As with the ML model, purchase
rhythm features (`purchase_frequency`, `active_months`, `recency`,
`tenure_days`) are the strongest, most significant predictors.

**These coefficients describe association, not causation** — e.g. we
cannot claim that artificially increasing a customer's `purchase_frequency`
would cause the corresponding increase in propensity, since frequency may
itself reflect unobserved product fit or satisfaction.

## Final Customer Dataset

`src/build_final_dataset.py` produces
`data/processed/final_customer_dataset.csv` with columns: `customer_id,
segment, recency, frequency, monetary_value, AOV, purchase_probability`.
Segments are assigned using full-history features; the propensity score
comes from a model trained on the leakage-safe historical window and then
applied to each customer's current features.

## Dashboards

`src/generate_dashboard.py` builds a **single self-contained HTML file**,
`dashboard/customer_dashboard.html`, with four tabbed dashboards (Customer
Overview, Customer Segmentation, Purchase Propensity, Customer Behavior).
Just open it in any web browser - no server or internet connection needed.

## Limitations

- **Synthetic data**: absolute metrics are illustrative; the methodology
  transfers, the specific numbers don't.
- The propensity model applies only to **existing** customers with
  pre-cutoff history — it is not a new-customer acquisition/conversion
  model.
- Segmentation uses only transaction-derived behavioral features; adding
  true demographic, channel, or acquisition-source data would likely
  sharpen segment boundaries further.
- K-Means assumes roughly spherical clusters in the scaled feature space;
  a density-based method (e.g. DBSCAN) or Gaussian Mixture Models could
  reveal different structure.
- statsmodels coefficients describe statistical association conditional on
  the included features, not a proven causal effect.

## Future Improvements

- Try Gaussian Mixture Models or hierarchical clustering as an alternative
  segmentation and compare stability.
- Extend the propensity model with gradient-boosted trees (kept out of
  scope here per the project's allowed tech stack) for a non-linear
  comparison against logistic regression.
- Build a small "next best category" recommendation using each customer's
  purchase history.
- Track segment migration over time (which customers move from Champions
  to At-Risk, and why).

## Interview Questions

1. **Why generate synthetic data instead of using a public dataset?**
   Full control over realistic data-quality issues and the ability to embed
   known customer archetypes to sanity-check whether segmentation/modeling
   recovers sensible structure.
2. **Walk me through your RFM feature engineering.** Recency, frequency,
   monetary value, plus extensions like category diversity and purchase
   frequency per active month, all computed from a single `groupby` on
   cleaned transaction data as of a snapshot date.
3. **Why log-transform monetary_value before clustering?** K-Means uses
   Euclidean distance; heavily right-skewed spend would let a few whales
   dominate the distance calculation and distort cluster assignment.
4. **Why not just pick K=4 or K=5 because it "feels right"?** That's
   exactly what this project avoids — K is chosen by comparing silhouette
   scores across a range of candidates, and I explicitly document the
   trade-off when a business preference might justify a different K than
   the statistically optimal one.
5. **What does silhouette score measure?** How similar each point is to its
   own cluster compared to the next-nearest cluster; higher values mean
   better-separated, more cohesive clusters.
6. **How did you avoid data leakage in the propensity model?** Features use
   only transactions on/before a fixed cutoff date; the label uses only
   transactions strictly after that cutoff within a 30-day window — the two
   windows never overlap.
7. **Why a temporal split here but a time-based split by date range in the
   pricing project?** Both are temporal because propensity is inherently
   about predicting the future from the past. Here, the "split" is baked
   into the label definition itself (feature window vs. label window)
   rather than partitioning many rows into `train`/`test` blocks.
8. **Why Logistic Regression instead of a more complex classifier?**
   Interpretable coefficients (with p-values and confidence intervals via
   statsmodels) that map directly to business questions like "does recency
   matter more than frequency?", plus it's within this project's intended
   scope of foundational, explainable methods.
9. **Why report precision/recall/F1/ROC-AUC instead of accuracy?**
   The positive class isn't wildly imbalanced here, but accuracy still
   hides the trade-off between false positives and false negatives, which
   matters for deciding a business action threshold.
10. **What's the business cost asymmetry between false positives and false
    negatives here?** A false positive costs a low-value marketing touch; a
    false negative misses a chance to reinforce or convert a likely
    purchase — see the notebook's Section 11 for the full discussion.
11. **What would you change about the decision threshold, and why?**
    Depending on the cost asymmetry above, I'd likely lower the
    probability threshold below 0.5 to trade some precision for recall,
    tuned against the ROC curve or a precision-recall curve.
12. **How would you validate that your segments are actually useful?**
    Track whether segment membership predicts real business outcomes going
    forward (e.g. do "Champions" actually retain better, do "At-Risk" churn
    at a higher rate) rather than treating segmentation as an end in
    itself.
13. **What's a limitation of using K-Means here?** It assumes clusters are
    roughly spherical/similarly sized in the scaled feature space, and
    requires specifying K in advance (even though we chose it
    systematically rather than arbitrarily).
14. **How is this different from a churn model?** This propensity model
    predicts a short-term positive purchase event (next 30 days); a churn
    model typically predicts the absence of activity over a longer horizon
    and often uses survival-analysis techniques.
15. **How would you extend this to a real production setting** (even though
    this project is intentionally development-ready)? Schedule a periodic
    refresh of the cutoff date, monitor for feature/label drift, and
    re-validate the model's performance on each new rolling window before
    trusting its scores.
